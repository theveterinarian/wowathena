Court-ordered anger management classes are as long as your court or probation order says they are. Georgia does not set one standard number of hours or sessions for every case, so the answer is written in your own paperwork, or it comes from your probation officer.

That can feel frustrating when you just want a number. The good news is that finding your number usually takes one careful read of your order and, if needed, one phone call. This page shows you where to look, what the common wordings mean, how to count your deadline, and what happens if you miss it.

Who decides how many hours of anger management you need

In Georgia, the judge who sentences you sets the conditions of your sentence, and your probation officer helps you carry them out. The Georgia Department of Community Supervision (DCS) says a court may impose any special conditions it finds necessary and appropriate, depending on the nature and severity of the crime, and that people on supervision may be required to undergo rehabilitative services as directed by their officer.

Anger management is one of those conditions. Because it's set case by case, two people in the same county can be ordered to different lengths of class. A number you saw online may not match yours.

Why there's no state number. Georgia has state certification systems for some court-related classes. The Georgia Department of Driver Services (DDS) certifies programs such as DUI school and Driver Improvement, and Family Violence Intervention Programs (FVIPs) are certified by DCS with help from the Georgia Commission on Family Violence. Anger management classes aren't on any of those lists. That's why your court or probation officer, not a state rule, decides what counts.

Common order wordings and what they mean

Find the part of your sentence or probation paperwork that lists your "terms and conditions." Look for the words "anger management," a number of hours or sessions, a provider, and a deadline. The State Court of Athens-Clarke County sentence form, for example, lists anger management as a checkbox on the same line as substance abuse, FVIP and mental health, with a blank for where to go and a blank for "within ___ days of today/release." How to find your number, step by step

  1. Get your sentence sheet or probation order. You should have received a copy at sentencing. If you didn't, ask the clerk of the court that sentenced you. In Athens-Clarke County, that's the Clerk of Superior and State Courts at 325 E. Washington St., Suite 450, 706-613-3190, for a State Court case, or Municipal Court at 325 E. Washington St., Suite 170, 706-613-3690, for a Municipal Court case.
  2. Find the "terms and conditions of probation" section. Only the boxes that are checked apply to you.
  3. Find the anger management line. Note every detail on it: hours or sessions, a provider name, the word "evaluation," and the deadline.
  4. Check for related lines. Look for a line that ends probation early once you show proof, and for any other condition that must be done first.
  5. If anything is blank, call your probation officer. In Athens-Clarke County, misdemeanor probation is supervised by ACC Probation Services at 110 Bray Street, 706-613-3911, Monday through Friday from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. If you're not on supervised probation, call the clerk instead.
  6. Write down the answer, with the date and the name of the person you spoke with. Then match a class to it before you pay.

What your deadline means and how to count it

Where the clock starts

On the Athens-Clarke County form, the deadline reads "within ___ days of today/release." "Today" is the day the judge signs the sentence. "Release" is the day you're released from any confinement that's part of the sentence, so if you served a few days in jail first, the two dates differ. The form doesn't say whether the count includes every calendar day or only business days, so ask your probation officer and plan as if every day counts.

Counting backward

Your deadline is the day proof must be in your officer's hands, not the day you finish class. Working backward, you need time for four things: registering, waiting for the next class to start, attending every session, and getting the certificate delivered.

A simple example, with made-up numbers: if your order gives you 90 days and the class meets once a week for 8 weeks, the class alone takes about 56 days. Add a week or two for registration and the wait for the next start date, plus a few days for the certificate, and there's little cushion left for a missed session. Register in the first week or two after sentencing, not the last month before the deadline.

What happens if you miss the deadline

Every checked condition on your sentence is a condition of probation, and missing it is a probation violation. The Athens-Clarke County State Court sentence form spells out the risk in the line you sign: if you violate probation in any way, you may be arrested and may have to serve the balance of your probation sentence in jail.

What actually happens in your case is up to your court and your probation officer. What you can control is how early you deal with a problem. If a class won't finish in time, tell your officer before the deadline and ask whether more time is possible; only the court can decide that. If you missed a session, ask the provider about a makeup right away and tell your officer the new finish date.

Finishing early can help. The Athens-Clarke County form has a line that lets probation end, or become unsupervised, once you show proof of FVIP or anger management and any other listed items. If that box is checked, finishing sooner may mean finishing probation sooner.

FVIP is not anger management

If your order says "Family Violence Intervention Program" or "FVIP," an anger management class will not satisfy it. Georgia requires a certified FVIP. These are 24-week programs certified by DCS, with the Georgia Commission on Family Violence (GCFV) helping to train, certify and monitor them. Georgia's FVIP rules say a program may not give FVIP credit for attending an anger management class. You can find certified programs in the directory at gcfv.georgia.gov. Athena does not offer FVIP.

GCFV explains the difference this way: anger management treats anger as the trigger for violence, while FVIPs treat family violence as learned behavior used to control a partner or family member. GCFV also quotes O.C.G.A. 19-13-16(a): when a court sentences someone for an offense involving family violence, it must order an FVIP unless it states on the record why that isn't appropriate.

Because the length of an FVIP is set by rule, it's the one program here with a fixed answer. Georgia's Rule 105-3-.07 requires the following.

FVIP requirementWhat the rule says
Number of classesAt least 24 group classes, one per week. Intake orientation doesn't count toward the 24.
Length of each classAt least 90 minutes and no more than 120 minutes of instruction. Attendance and fee collection can't eat into the 90 minutes.
Finishing fasterNot allowed. Participants may not attend more than one class per week to speed up completion, apart from one approved makeup class in the same week.
AbsencesNo more than 3. A participant must be terminated after the fourth absence. Arriving late means no credit for that class, and each late arrival after 3 counts as an absence.
FeesNo more than $60 per class and no more than $120 for orientation and intake. GCFV says classes average $25 to $30, and programs must have a sliding scale for people the court declares indigent.
FormatIn person or online, on an approved video platform with cameras on.
Proof of completionFVIPs don't issue certificates. They must provide a verification letter on request and notify the court and other referral sources within 4 days of completion.
Credit for other classesNone. An FVIP may not give FVIP credit for an anger management, DUI or any other class.

If your case involved family violence and you're unsure which program you were ordered to attend, ask your probation officer or your attorney before you enroll anywhere.

Questions to ask your probation officer or the clerk

A short call now can save you from paying for the wrong class. Write down the answers, the date and the name of the person you spoke with.

  • How many hours or sessions do I need? Ask for the exact number, not a range.
  • What's my deadline, and which date does it run from? Sentencing or release.
  • Does it have to be a certain type of class? Ask whether the class needs a live instructor, a group format or a provider they've approved.
  • Can I take it live online, or does it have to be in person? We cover this in detail in Can you take anger management classes online in Georgia?
  • Do you need to approve the provider first? If so, ask how to send them the class details.
  • How should proof reach you? Ask whether the certificate should go to the court, the probation office or the clerk, and whether you should bring a copy yourself.
  • Does finishing early change anything? Some sentences end probation, or make it unsupervised, once proof is in.

How to confirm a class before you pay

Once you know what your order requires, check the class against it. Call or email the provider and ask them these things directly.

  1. How many hours is the class, and how many sessions? Make sure the total meets your order.
  2. Is it taught live by an instructor, or is it self-paced? If your officer wants a live class, a video course may not count.
  3. What does the certificate of completion show? It should show your name, the class name, the hours completed and the completion date.
  4. Can they send the certificate to your court or probation officer? Some providers do this for you.
  5. When does the next class start, and when would I finish? Compare the finish date with your deadline.

If anything doesn't line up with what your probation officer told you, check with the officer before you pay. Remember that the hours in your order are only half of "how long will this take?" The other half is how often the class meets, so ask for a finish date, not just a total. It also helps to know what the sessions will cover. How to document that you finished

Ask for the certificate of completion as soon as the last session ends, and check it before you leave. It should show your full legal name as it appears on your court paperwork, the name of the class, the total hours or sessions, the completion date, and the provider's name and contact details. Then keep these together until your probation ends, and preferably longer.

  • Your certificate of completion. Keep the original or a clear digital copy.
  • Your receipt or enrollment confirmation. It shows when you signed up.
  • Notes from calls with your probation officer. Include dates and what you were told about hours and format.
  • Confirmation that the certificate was received. If the provider sends it for you, ask them to let you know when it's sent, and follow up with your officer.

Key terms

  • Terms and conditions of probation. The list of things your sentence requires you to do or avoid. Only the items checked or written in apply to you.
  • Special condition. A condition a court adds because of the nature of the case, according to DCS. Anger management and treatment are examples.
  • Probation officer. The person who supervises your probation and, under DCS's standard conditions, can direct you to rehabilitative services.
  • Certificate of completion. The document a class provider issues when you finish. For anger management, its contents aren't set by a Georgia rule, so check that it has what your officer needs.
  • "Within ___ days of today/release." Deadline wording: proof must reach the court or your officer within that many days of sentencing or of release.
  • Evaluation and treatment. Order wording that means an assessment first, then whatever treatment or classes the evaluator recommends.
  • Family Violence Intervention Program (FVIP). A 24-week program certified by DCS for offenses involving family violence. Not interchangeable with anger management.
  • Verification letter. What an FVIP provides to show classes attended or completion. Under Georgia's rule, FVIPs don't issue certificates.
  • Probation violation. Failing to meet any condition of probation. The Athens-Clarke County form warns it can lead to arrest and jail time for the balance of the sentence.
