In anger management classes, you learn practical skills for handling anger before it turns into a problem: how to spot your triggers and early warning signs, how to calm your body down, how to change the thoughts that fuel anger, and how to speak up and solve conflicts without a blowup. It's a skills class, and most of the time is spent learning and practicing those tools.

If a court or probation officer sent you, or you signed up on your own, it's normal to feel a little defensive walking in. You won't be told that you're a bad person or that you should never feel angry. The focus is on what you do next when anger shows up.

What anger management classes are

Anger management is a structured class, usually in a group, that teaches you to control how you respond to anger. There's no official Georgia anger management curriculum, so classes differ. A common approach is cognitive-behavioral, which focuses on the link between what you think, what you feel and what you do.

One well-known model comes from SAMHSA, the federal Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, which publishes a 12-week cognitive-behavioral anger management program. It starts with three definitions that come up in almost every class:

  • Anger is a feeling. It ranges from mild irritation to intense rage.
  • Aggression is behavior meant to hurt someone or damage property.
  • Hostility is a set of attitudes and judgments that push a person toward aggression.

That split matters. Feeling angry isn't the problem. SAMHSA's workbook says anger becomes a problem when it's felt too intensely, felt too often, or expressed in the wrong way.

Recognizing your triggers and warning signs

The first skill is noticing. You learn to identify the events that tend to set you off, such as waiting a long time, being stuck in traffic, someone saying something hurtful or untrue, or an inconsiderate neighbor. Classes often call your most sensitive topics "red flags," because they can spark anger fast.

Next, you learn to catch the warning signs that anger is building. SAMHSA's model sorts them into four types:

Type of cueWhat it looks like
PhysicalA faster heartbeat, a tight chest, feeling hot or flushed.
BehavioralClenching your fists, raising your voice, staring someone down.
EmotionalOther feelings under the anger, such as fear, hurt, jealousy or feeling disrespected.
ThoughtHostile self-talk, or picturing getting even.

Many classes use an anger meter to track this. It's a 1-to-10 scale, where 1 is completely calm and 10 is losing control. You rate your anger each day. Over time you start to see your patterns and notice a 5 or a 6 before it becomes a 10.

Calming techniques you practice in class

Once you can see anger building, you need ways to bring it down. Classes teach two kinds of tools: quick ones for the moment, and habits that lower your stress over time.

  • Timeouts. In its simplest form, a timeout means taking a few deep breaths and thinking before you react. It can also mean leaving the room and agreeing to come back to the conversation later. Some people set up a timeout plan with family members in advance.
  • Deep breathing. Slow, full breaths help your body settle. SAMHSA's workbook notes that even three deep breaths can help when anger is rising, and that it works best if you practice it often.
  • Progressive muscle relaxation. You tense and then relax muscle groups one at a time, from your hands up to your face, so you learn what "relaxed" feels like.
  • Longer-term habits. Regular exercise and support from people you trust are part of many anger control plans.

These tools add up to an anger control plan, your personal list of what to do when you feel anger building. Some instructors call it a toolbox.

Changing the thoughts that fuel anger

A big idea in anger management is that events don't make you angry on their own. What you tell yourself about the event does. If a driver cuts you off and you think "he did that on purpose to disrespect me," your anger climbs faster than if you think "he probably didn't see me."

SAMHSA's program teaches this with the A-B-C-D model:

  1. A, the activating event: what happened.
  2. B, your beliefs: what you told yourself about it.
  3. C, the consequence: how you felt because of those beliefs.
  4. D, dispute: checking whether those beliefs are realistic, and swapping in a more accurate way to see it.

Another tool is thought stopping. Instead of arguing with the angry thought, you tell yourself to drop it, with something like "Don't go there" or "I'll only get into trouble if I keep thinking this way."

Communicating and solving problems without a fight

Classes also teach you how to stand up for yourself without aggression. You learn the difference between three styles:

  • Aggressive: my feelings matter and yours don't.
  • Passive: your feelings matter and mine don't, which often leads to resentment.
  • Assertive: my feelings matter and so do yours.

SAMHSA's model then walks through a five-step Conflict Resolution Model: name the problem, name your feelings, name the specific impact, decide whether it's worth raising, and then set a time to talk it through calmly and work out a fix. You practice with real examples from your own life.

Some programs also look at how anger was handled in the family you grew up in, since many of us learned our habits from the adults around us. And many classes clear up common myths, like the idea that "venting" by yelling or punching a pillow is healthy. SAMHSA's workbook says research shows people who vent aggressively just get better at being angry.

What anger management classes are like

Details vary by provider, but here is what to expect in many classes:

  • A small group with an instructor. You learn from others' examples as well as your own.
  • Ground rules. Common ones are no threats or violence in class and keeping what others share confidential.
  • A check-in. Sessions often start with each person sharing their highest anger meter number that week and what helped.
  • Practice between sessions. You try a skill during the week and report back.
  • Attendance rules. Missing sessions can cost you your certificate. In SAMHSA's model, someone who misses more than 3 of the 12 sessions doesn't receive one. Ask about your class's makeup policy up front.

SAMHSA's model runs as 12 weekly group sessions, but that's one model, not a Georgia rule. If you're taking a class for court, the length you need comes from your order. Why these skills matter beyond the class

The obvious benefit is staying out of trouble. SAMHSA's workbook lists what can follow an explosion of anger: jail, restitution, losing a job, and losing family and friends, along with guilt and shame. The skills are meant to stop things before they reach that point.

There's a health side too. MedlinePlus, from the National Library of Medicine, notes that people who blow up in anger have a higher risk of heart attack and stroke. Learning to calm down is good for your body as well as your relationships.

What anger management is not

Anger management is not a Family Violence Intervention Program (FVIP). If your order says "Family Violence Intervention Program" or "FVIP," an anger management class will not satisfy it. Georgia requires a certified FVIP, a 24-week program certified by the Department of Community Supervision with help from the Georgia Commission on Family Violence (GCFV). Georgia's FVIP rules say an FVIP may not give FVIP credit for an anger management class. Find certified programs at gcfv.georgia.gov. Athena does not offer FVIP.

GCFV explains that the two programs start from different ideas. Anger management treats anger as the trigger for violence. FVIPs treat family violence as learned behavior used to control a partner or family member, so the approach is different.
