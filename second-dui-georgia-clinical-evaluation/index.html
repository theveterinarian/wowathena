Second DUI in Georgia: your license and the clinical evaluation

A second DUI changes what Georgia asks of you. Here's what DDS says happens to your license, why you'll need a clinical evaluation, and how DUI school, treatment and ignition interlock fit together.

By Athena DUI Academy & Family EnrichmentLast reviewed September 28, 20268 min read

Key takeaways

  • A second DUI within 5 years brings a license suspension of at least 18 months, with no driving at all for the first 120 days.
  • After the 120-day hard suspension, you may qualify for an ignition interlock limited permit and keep the device for 12 months.
  • Anyone with two or more DUI convictions within 10 years must complete a state-approved clinical evaluation, and any treatment it recommends, before DDS will reinstate the license.
  • A plea of nolo contendere counts as a conviction for the 10-year clinical evaluation rule.
  • You still need to finish a DDS-certified Risk Reduction Program (DUI school). The evaluation and DUI school are separate requirements.

A second DUI within 5 years in Georgia brings a license suspension of at least 18 months, and you can't drive at all for the first 120 days. And if you have two or more DUI convictions within 10 years, Georgia requires a clinical evaluation, plus any treatment it recommends, before DDS will give your license back.

That's a lot to take in at once. This page walks through what the Georgia Department of Driver Services (DDS) and the Department of Behavioral Health and Developmental Disabilities (DBHDD) say happens next, in the order you'll likely deal with it. Your criminal case is a separate matter decided by the court, and a DUI attorney is the right person to advise you on it.

What happens to your license after a second DUI in Georgia?

Georgia uses two look-back windows, and they do different jobs. The 5-year window decides how long your license is suspended. The 10-year window decides whether you need a clinical evaluation. Both are measured from the arrest date of the earlier conviction to the arrest date of the new one.

A plea of nolo contendere doesn't get you around the clinical evaluation rule. Georgia law counts a nolo plea to DUI, and any accepted nolo pleas in the past 10 years, as convictions for this purpose.

Who needs a clinical evaluation after a DUI in Georgia?

DDS requires documented proof of a state-approved clinical evaluation from anyone who wants to reinstate a suspension that resulted from two or more DUI convictions within 10 years. The law behind this, O.C.G.A. § 40-5-63.1, also says that if the evaluation recommends treatment, you must complete it before your license is reinstated, and that both are at your own expense except in limited cases set by state law.

A court can also order an evaluation as part of a DUI sentence, including a first DUI. DBHDD, which runs the DUI Intervention Program, says DUI offenders are required to attend DUI school, have a clinical evaluation and complete any treatment the evaluator recommends. Your sentencing paperwork tells you what the court ordered in your case.

What happens at a DUI clinical evaluation?

A clinical evaluation is the process Georgia uses to diagnose a substance use problem and, if needed, refer you to treatment. It isn't a class or a test you pass or fail. Under DBHDD's rules:

  • Only approved evaluators can do it. The evaluator must be licensed or certified, approved by DBHDD and listed on its registry, and the evaluation must take place at an approved location.
  • It starts with a face-to-face interview. The evaluator asks about your background, medical history, the alcohol concentration from your current offense, your social and family history, your substance use history, your work background and your mental status.
  • It uses your DUI school screening. The evaluator reviews the standardized screening you took in the Risk Reduction Program and uses at least one other assessment approved by DBHDD.
  • It ends with a recommendation. The evaluator either rules out a substance-related disorder or recommends a level of treatment, and writes a report within seven days of the interview.

If treatment is recommended, the evaluator gives you a list of approved treatment providers for that level of care. If no treatment is recommended, the evaluation goes to DBHDD for review. DBHDD aims to finish its review within two weeks, and if it approves, it sends a Requirements Met Certificate directly to you. Our page on the DUI clinical evaluation in Athens covers what to expect on the day.

Do you have to finish the recommended treatment?

Yes. For two or more DUIs in 10 years, DDS won't reinstate your license until you submit proof that you completed any treatment your evaluation recommended. DBHDD's rules set out how that works:

  • You must stay in treatment for at least the time your evaluator recommended. A provider can require more, but someone who has followed their treatment plan can't be required to stay longer than one year.
  • Longer-term treatment means at least three hours a week. You may also be asked to attend 12-step or other self-help meetings, but that time doesn't count toward the three hours.
  • You have to use a DBHDD-approved provider that offers the level of care you were referred to.
  • If more than 60 days pass between your evaluation and starting treatment, the provider may re-evaluate you.
  • When you finish, the provider gives you a certificate of treatment completion. You may have to pay all treatment fees before you receive it.

Where DUI school fits in

The clinical evaluation doesn't replace DUI school. You need both. Georgia's DUI statute lists completion of a DUI Alcohol or Drug Use Risk Reduction Program within 120 days of conviction as part of the sentence for a second conviction within 10 years, and DDS requires your Risk Reduction certificate before it issues an interlock permit or reinstates your license.

The program costs $360 at every DDS-certified school. It includes a 130-question assessment and a 20-hour course, and DDS doesn't accept certificates from online DUI courses. Because your evaluator reviews the screening from DUI school, it's worth enrolling early. Our complete guide to Georgia DUI school explains how the program works.

Ignition interlock after a second DUI

For a second DUI within 5 years, DDS says you have no driving privileges for the first 120 days. After that hard suspension, you may be eligible for an ignition interlock limited permit and must keep a certified device in any vehicle you drive for 12 months. According to the DDS Traffic Court Reference Manual, to get the interlock permit you need:

  • Your Risk Reduction Program certificate of completion
  • Proof that you enrolled in or completed DBHDD-approved treatment, if your clinical evaluation required it (or permission from the court if your case is in a DUI court program)
  • Proof that an ignition interlock device was installed by a DDS-approved provider
  • A $25 permit fee

To reinstate your full license later, DDS looks for proof that the device was kept in your vehicle for 12 months without incident, proof that you finished any recommended treatment (or a Requirements Met Certificate if no treatment was recommended) and payment of the reinstatement fee. A sentencing court can exempt you from the interlock for undue financial hardship, but then you serve a 12-month hard suspension. DDS notes that drivers who aren't Georgia residents serve an 18-month hard suspension instead of the interlock period.

For the details on devices and permits, see our pages on ignition interlock in Georgia and the Georgia limited driving permit after a DUI.

What about jail, fines and the court case?

Georgia's DUI law sets tougher penalties for a second conviction within 10 years than for a first. The court decides your sentence, and it depends on the facts of your case and your record. We can't tell you what your sentence will be, and neither can any DUI school. Talk to a Georgia DUI attorney before your court date.

What to do next

  1. Check your DDS record. Sign in to DDS Online Services to see your personal reinstatement requirements, or call DDS at 678-413-8400.
  2. Talk to an attorney about your court case.
  3. Enroll in a DDS-certified Risk Reduction Program so you meet the 120-day deadline and have your certificate ready.
  4. Schedule your clinical evaluation with a DBHDD-approved evaluator.
  5. Start and finish any recommended treatment, and keep your certificate of treatment completion.
  6. Apply for the interlock permit once you're eligible, then reinstate when your suspension ends. Our guide to getting your license back after a DUI walks through that step.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if you get 2 DUIs in Georgia?

If both arrests fall within 5 years, DDS suspends your license for at least 18 months, with no driving for the first 120 days. Because you have two DUIs within 10 years, you also need a clinical evaluation and any recommended treatment, plus DUI school, before DDS will reinstate your license. Your court decides the criminal sentence, so speak with a DUI attorney.

Do I need a clinical evaluation if my first DUI was more than 5 years ago?

Yes, if both convictions fall within 10 years. Georgia measures the 10 years from the arrest date of the earlier conviction to the arrest date of the new one. The 5-year window affects how long your license is suspended, but the clinical evaluation rule looks back 10 years.

How long does DUI treatment take in Georgia?

It depends on what your clinical evaluator recommends. Under DBHDD rules you must stay in treatment at least as long as recommended, and someone who follows the treatment plan can't be required to stay longer than one year. Longer-term treatment is at least three hours a week.

Can I drive after a second DUI in Georgia?

Not for the first 120 days after a second DUI within 5 years. After that, you may qualify for an ignition interlock limited permit if you have finished DUI school, enrolled in or finished any recommended treatment, and installed an approved device. Check your exact requirements with DDS.

Does a nolo contendere plea count as a DUI for the 10-year rule?

Yes. Georgia's clinical evaluation law says a nolo contendere plea to DUI, and any accepted nolo pleas within the past 10 years, are counted as convictions for this rule.

Who pays for the clinical evaluation and treatment?

In most cases you do. Georgia law says the evaluation and treatment are at your own expense, with limited exceptions set by state law. At Athena DUI Academy in Athens, a clinical evaluation is $150, and any treatment is paid separately to the treatment provider.
